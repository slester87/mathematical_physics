What the window tells us

Two simulated photons arrive in the first two seconds with reconstructed energies between 1.66 and 1.84 MeV. The expected background is one count. If the merger emitted 1.2 × 1052 escaping photons, which model lines would have produced too many counts here?

At √σ = 1.0 MeV, the SU(3) benchmark predicts 8.05 signal counts, in addition to background. The chance of counting two photons or fewer is 1.11%. Raising √σ to 1.1 MeV moves its mean line energy from 1.686 to 1.854 MeV. The same yield then gives a lower-tail probability of 31.55%.

Evidence

The merger, host spectrum, detector, and photon counts are synthetic. The three scalar mass ratios come from published continuum lattice calculations.

Why the group remains ambiguous

For a scalar glueball at rest that decays into two photons, the observed line energy depends on a product. Write rN for the lattice scalar mass in units of the square root of the string tension. At host redshift z,

Eγ = rN √σ / [2(1 + z)].

A change in √σ can compensate for a change in gauge group. SU(2) at 0.9 MeV and SU(3) at 1.0 MeV place their lines only about 1 keV apart. The instrument’s assumed resolution is 25 keV. Distinguishing groups would require additional information, such as a second identified line whose energy ratio cancels the common scale.

Model assumptions

The benchmark assumes prompt, isotropic emission through a two-photon decay channel. The escaping photon yield is a free parameter. These conditional exclusions establish no observational constraint on a gauge group or on the Yang–Mills mass gap.

Reproduce the calculation

The analysis reweights the gravitational-wave distance and redshift samples using the host line centroid. It then averages the Poisson lower tail over that distribution and the lattice uncertainty. Increasing the quadrature order from 48 to 96 preserves all nine decisions.

python3 -m pip install -r requirements.txt
